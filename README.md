# Hoops Dynasty

A native Android college basketball coaching game, written in Kotlin with Jetpack Compose.

Pick a school, set your rotation and tactics, play or sim games, recruit high schoolers and transfers, manage NIL money, develop players, and chase national titles season after season.

You can coach any of the 364 Division I schools, in their real 2026-27 conferences (32 of them, to the best of our knowledge), with real names, nicknames and colors. Every player and coach is fictional. Every school shows its own logo (the logo files you supplied, in `app/src/main/assets/logos`, one per team named by its abbreviation). A school without a logo file falls back to a drawn monogram in its colors. The logos are the schools' trademarks, so keep this build for personal use and don't publish it to an app store. To fix any school's name, nickname or colors, use **Career → Settings → Schools**.

The design is clean and neutral, with light and dark modes, and takes its accent color from the school you coach.

## This branch: Pro career (experiment)

The `pro-career` branch grows out of Road to the League: when your player is drafted, his career keeps going in a pro league. It builds as its own app, **Hoops Dynasty Pro** (`com.nh.hoopscoach.pro`), so it installs next to the main game and the Road app without touching their saves. Everything below about Road to the League still applies up to Draft Night.

- **Draft Night to the pros**: the board shows which pro team took each player (by roster strength, with a lottery among the four worst). After your name is called, "Start your pro career" builds the league. An undrafted senior rated 86 or better gets a two-way deal instead of going overseas.
- **The league**: the 30 real teams in the East and West, with their logos, and their current players with headshots (522 players; the logos and photos are the user's own files, for personal use, in `assets/nba`). Overalls and ages are estimates on the game's pro scale (`game/NbaData.kt`, generated from the roster file). Each team keeps its best 14; your draft class (your college classmates, and your rival if he's been drafted) joins as rookies, weakest rosters picking first. **The pros are a step up: a college player arrives at least 5 lower and no higher than 87** (80 on the 2K scale the screens show; a 92 starts at 87), and **no rookie goes above 87 during his first season**: summer camp and practice still sharpen the skills he works on, but his overall holds at 87 until his first summer. His ceiling stays, so rookies earn their minutes and grow into stars. Pro coaches give young, high-ceiling players development minutes (up to 4 points of credit in the rotation). Future drafts bring rookies by pick: about 85 for No. 1 (77 on screen, up to 80), 80 for No. 30 (72), 76 for No. 60, with real upside. Over the years real players age and retire, and generated rookies take their places. Coaches are generated.
- **The season**: 83 games: everyone twice (58), twice more against 12 of the 14 conference rivals (24), and **Rivalry Night** (game 83) against a natural rival (Celtics-Lakers, Knicks-Nets, Bulls-Pistons and so on). Live games, Spotlight, big moments, the press, practice, staff and award races all carry over. Playoff stats are kept apart from the regular season.
- **The NBA calendar**:
  - *NBA Cup*: six groups of five (three per conference, drawn from five tiers of strength). Each team plays its group mates once on five Cup nights in the first month; those games count in the standings. Group winners and the best runner-up in each conference reach the quarterfinals; the semifinals and Final are in Las Vegas. Knockout games are extra and don't count in the standings or season stats, so everyone still plays 83. The winners get "NBA Cup winner", one gets Cup MVP, and your player earns prize money ($51K to $515K by round). The League tab's Cup page shows the bracket and the group tables; Today shows where you stand.
  - *All-Star Weekend*: the Rising Stars (first- and second-year players) with an MVP, the 3-Point Contest (eight shooters, a final three, scored out of 34), the Slam Dunk Contest (four young finishers, a final two), then the All-Star Game (twelve per conference; your followers count as fan votes). Taking part gains followers; winning gains more.
  - *Play-in*: seeds 1 to 6 are in. Seeds 7 to 10 play single games: 7 vs 8 (the winner is the 7 seed), 9 vs 10 (the loser goes home), then the 7-8 loser hosts the 9-10 winner for the 8 seed. Then best-of-seven through the Finals.
  - *Awards night*: 100 voters rank five MVP candidates 10-7-5-3-1. Today shows the winner, his first-place votes, the top five, the other major awards and where you finished.
  - *Lottery night*: the 14 teams that miss the playoffs draw for the top four picks with real odds (14% for the three worst records, down to 0.5%). The Draft step shows the order and who moved up or down.
- **Real rules**: the game engine plays each level by its rulebook.
  - *NBA*: four 12-minute quarters (box scores and the live game go by quarter), a 24-second shot clock (14 after an offensive rebound), six fouls to foul out, and the penalty (two shots) from a team's fifth foul in a quarter, its fourth in overtime, or its second in the last two minutes of a period. Team fouls reset every quarter. Offensive fouls count against the player, not the team. The team that loses the opening tip starts the 2nd and 3rd quarters with the ball.
  - *College*: two 20-minute halves, a 30-second shot clock (20 after an offensive rebound), five fouls to foul out, a one-and-one from a team's seventh foul in a half and two shots from the tenth. Fouls reset at halftime and carry into overtime. Offensive fouls count as team fouls.
  - *Both*: five-minute overtimes; fouling late is only a side out until the penalty, so trailing teams keep fouling; nobody fouls after fouling out, and coaches sit starters in foul trouble (two in the first half or four in the second in college; in the NBA, one more than the quarter). Free throws run about 78% in the NBA and 71% in college; a pro team commits about 19 fouls and shoots 22 free throws a game, a college team about 17 and 19. `RulesTest` plays hundreds of games at each level and checks the periods, minutes, foul limits and penalty rules.
- **Menus**: every drop-down (the "Sim ahead" menus on Today and the pickers for game plans, play styles and home state) is a rounded card with a heading, icons, and a line under each choice. The sim menus say what each jump skips ("39 games away", "26 regular-season games left"); the pro menu adds "Sim to the trade deadline", which stops a day early so you can still ask for a trade. Pickers check the current choice, highlight while open, open at least as wide as the field, and long lists open scrolled to your pick.
- **Era mode**: when you go pro, pick the league you join: **Today**, **2013** (LeBron's Heat), **2003** (Shaq, Kobe and Duncan), **1996** (Jordan's Bulls) or **1986** (Bird and Magic). After Draft Night, "Next: choose your league" opens the going-pro screen with the era picker (before 1.5 the Draft Night button skipped it and always started today's league).
  - *Players*: each past era is built from the user's NBA eras pack (`resources/eras`, made by `tools/build_eras.py`; personal use only): the top 120 players by Win Shares that season on their real teams (the team they started the season with), plus a few stars who missed most of it (Jordan in 1985-86, Derrick Rose in 2012-13) from the earlier estimates. Ratings come from each player's NBA 2K card (the 2K3 and 2K13 launch ratings for 2003 and 2013; 2K27 classic, all-decade or all-time cards for 1986 and 1996), blended with his Win Shares rank and placed on the game's pro scale (Bird, Jordan, Shaq and LeBron 99 in their eras). Where the card has attributes, the player keeps his shape: McHale is a post player who rebounds and blocks shots, Bird a shooter and passer. About 120 players per era have their photo (about 580 headshots). Generated players fill out the rosters.
  - *Start in an era*: the player creator has an **Era** choice (Today, 2013, 2003, 1996, 1986). Pick 1986 and college starts in 1985-86 with the real future pros at their real schools (Brad Daugherty at North Carolina, David Robinson at Navy, Len Bias at Maryland; about 160 a season, everyone who went in the first 60 picks of a draft from 1986 on, matched to the game's schools). Each new season brings that year's real freshmen. Their ratings follow where and when they were drafted (a future No. 1 pick is about 95 in his last college season, a No. 60 about 88), and in-game growth does the rest, so they go in the college draft about where they really did, mixed in with made-up players. Draft Night names that year's NBA teams. When he turns pro, the going-pro screen shows his era's league (no other choice), and the years he spent in school pass for the pros: the 1985-86 stars are four years older, retirements happen, and the real 1986, 1987 and 1988 draft classes have arrived (David Robinson, Scottie Pippen, Mitch Richmond...), each team keeping its first-round picks. His own class comes from the college draft he was part of. A career that chose Today in the creator can still pick a past era when it goes pro, as before.
  - *Real teams by year*: an era league has only the teams that existed: 23 in 1985-86, then the Heat and Hornets in 1988-89, the Timberwolves and Magic in 1989-90, the Raptors and Vancouver Grizzlies in 1995-96, the Hornets' move to New Orleans in 2002-03 (they keep their players), and the Charlotte Bobcats in 2004-05, back to 30. Names change in their real years (Bullets to Wizards, Vancouver to Memphis, Sonics to Thunder, New Jersey to Brooklyn, Hornets to Pelicans, Bobcats to Hornets), with the old name on a badge in the old colors and today's logo when the name is today's. Each new team stocks its roster in an expansion draft: teams protect their best eight and lose at most one player, and the news lists every pick. Membership follows the real expansions and moves; names come from the eras pack year by year.
  - *Schedules*: with an odd number of teams nobody can play 83, so those seasons play 82 like the real league (everyone twice, then more games against conference rivals, spread over them). A 30-team season keeps the 83-game schedule.
  - *How they play*: era players with a 2K card keep its strengths; the rest (most of 2003 and 2013, where the data has overalls only) get strengths built from their real numbers that season (Durant's 28 points make him a scorer, Rondo's assists a passer, Howard's rebounds a big who crashes the glass), and every real player, then and today, gets the play style that fits his strengths, so the stars score like stars.
  - *Real drafts*: every summer of an era career brings that year's real draft class (the first 60 picks, 1986 to 2026), in the game's draft order: Daugherty and Bias in 1986, David Robinson and Scottie Pippen in 1987, Shaq in 1992, Iverson, Kobe and Nash in 1996, LeBron in 2003. Each comes in with his college (and his college seasons on his player page) and, if he later shows up on an era list, the ceiling he reached there (Pippen grows toward 97, Kobe toward 98) and his photo. Drafts with fewer than 60 picks fill out with generated players.
  - *Style*: pace, three-point volume, two-point shooting and fouls match the era. A test season averages about 110 points, 5 threes attempted and 30 free throws in 1986; 99 points and 18 threes in 1996; 97 points on .453 shooting in 2003; 101 points and 22 threes in 2013.
  - *Rules*: no play-in (the top eight go straight in), no NBA Cup, best-of-five first rounds in 1986 and 1996, no Rising Stars game before 1994, no 65-game award rule and no supermax. The lottery uses the era's odds (top three picks drawn; 25% for the worst record from 1994).
  - *Money*: every salary, the max, the minimum, the rookie scale and endorsements scale with the era's salary cap ($4.9M in 1986, $23.0M in 1996, $40.3M in 2003, $58.0M in 2013).
  - The career starts in the era's year and runs on from there under its rules; legends playing in your league count on the all-time list by what they do there. The League tab's History page shows the era and its rules.
- **Media**: interviews, stories and a social feed, in college and the pros.
  - *Interviews*: after games a named reporter (a national outlet or the local beat writer) asks about what just happened: a game-winner, the rival, a triple-double or career high, the award race, a win or losing streak, a slump, trade talk before the deadline, a contract year, the rookie learning curve, playing time, fatigue, the draft (college) or a tough loss, each with several wordings. Three answers: humble (teammates and fans like it), brash (followers, but teammates may not) or even. Bold answers and big topics become news stories ("Grant on trade talk: ..."), a few days apart.
  - *Stories*: big nights, game-winners, rivalry games and big playoff nights get a short game story (dateline, his line and shooting, the record or series, a quote from the coach, who else stood out), and every two weeks a column on the MVP or Player of the Year race. Tap a headline with "Read" to open the story, with a link to the box score.
  - *Social feed*: the team account posts every final with his line; fans, doubters, analysts, highlight accounts, teammates and the rival react to his games and quotes (a brash reputation draws more doubters, a humble one more love). Likes scale with his following. Today shows the latest three; "See all" opens the feed (the last 80 posts).
- **Everyone ages**: the real NBA rosters are the 2025-26 league, and the years you spend in college pass for them too: by the time you're drafted they've aged, developed or declined, the oldest have retired (LeBron James, Al Horford and Mike Conley in a two-year test) and draftees have taken their spots. College players have real ages (high school seniors 17 or 18, freshmen 18 or 19, and up from there) that rise every offseason, on the player page.
- **Playoffs page**: one round at a time (Play-In, Round 1, Semis, Conf Finals, Finals), opening on the current round, with East and West in their own sections. Every series says where it stands and where the next game is ("Hawks lead 1-0 · Game 2 at Orlando"); play-in games say what's at stake ("Winner: 7 seed · loser plays again"). Before the playoffs it explains the format and shows the matchups if the season ended today. On Today, your series comes first, right under your player card, with a dot per game (filled for a win) and the next game. While the play-in runs, a top-six seed shows as in and waiting, with its first-round opponent when it's known. A playoff game you play live counts like any other: one game per series per day. Postgame questions follow the series ("One more win and you're through"), and the standings split into Playoffs (top 6), Play-in (7 to 10) and Lottery.
- **Winning**: talent decides pro games. Pro ratings sit close together, so the same rating gap counts for more in the pros (shooting, defense, turnovers and rebounding), and coaches matter half as much as in college. The best teams win about 60 to 65 games and the worst 15 to 25, and a team's rating predicts its record well. Once your player is a star (90 overall), his team builds around him: it buys at the trade deadline instead of selling, goes after the best veteran it can afford, and each summer, if the rest of the roster lags the league, signs a good veteran for the weakest starting spot. A test career went from a 38-45 rookie team to 50-to-67-win seasons and three titles once the star arrived.
- **Screens, most important first**: Today puts your player, the next game, anything you need to act on (the press, a trade request, the deadline), then results and standings; league honors and the social feed (two posts) sit near the bottom, above the news. The Career tab leads with your own career (totals, season by season, your story, honors, highlight reel, records) and puts the league's all-time list last. Free-agent offers at the same salary sort by the bigger role, then the better team.
- **The college newspaper**: after a rivalry game, Today carries your school's own paper ("The North Carolina Daily"): the edition line, the rivalry's name, a headline as big as fits, the final with each side's prestige swing, a Top Performers box (each team's best line, yours marked), the series in your dynasty, and a link to the written story when there is one (the box score otherwise). Stories open as a newspaper page: paper and serif type, the outlet's nameplate, the headline, the byline and the story.
- **Pop-ups for big moments**: your awards-night honors (one pop-up with all of them, "You're the MVP" when it's the big one), Player of the Month, being named an All-Star (starter or reserve), getting traded, breaking a record, and winning the title (with Finals MVP); in college, every honor (All-Conference, All-America, Player of the Year...). They queue, so a long sim can't skip one, and show one at a time ("Next", then "Nice").
- **Trade value**: a player's value climbs steeply with his overall, peaks in his prime and falls fast after 30, adds upside while he's young, and now counts what he's producing this season against what his rating predicts. Each team sees value its own way: rebuilding teams pay about 20% more for players 24 and under and 25% less for 30 and over; contenders pay about 15% more for 27-to-33-year-olds who help now. Trade offers for your player say what kind of deal it is (Fair deal, They're overpaying, A little light, Lowball).
- **Open markets**: in college, every school at or below a player's level offers (a five-star can go anywhere, a four-star almost anywhere), and the transfer portal works the same way from his current rating. The offer list (and the portal) sorts by Top programs, Playing time, Most NIL or Near home, searches by school or conference, and shows 20 at a time. In pro free agency every team makes an offer: teams that need him at his position pay more, teams set there less; sort by Most money, Biggest role or Contenders.
- **Real accounts**: the social feed uses real accounts from the user's social accounts pack (`resources/social`, avatars in `assets/social`, made by `tools/build_social.py`; personal use): the team's own account posts its finals (@nuggets, @UNC_Basketball...), highlights and media posts come from ESPN, Bleacher Report, House of Highlights and the like, analysts are real (Zach Lowe in the pros; Jay Bilas, Dick Vitale, Gary Parrish, Ken Pomeroy, Joe Lunardi in college), rumors and breaking trades come from Shams Charania, Marc Stein or Chris Haynes (Jon Rothstein, Jeff Goodman, Pete Thamel and 247Sports, On3 and Rivals in college), and league honors from @NBA. Each shows its avatar and verified check. Fans and doubters stay made up, and past-era careers keep made-up accounts.
- **The rumor mill**: in the two weeks before the deadline, sources say who's shopping whom ("The Knicks have called the Jazz about ..."), in the news and from an NBA Insider account on the social feed; when the user's player is unhappy or has asked out, the rumors turn to him, and fans react. Every trade breaks on the Insider's feed first ("BREAKING: ..."), with fans of the user's team weighing in. When he hits free agency, the Insider reports how many teams made offers and the favorite.
- **League-wide free agency**: when the offseason reaches free agency, a year comes off every deal and players whose deals run out hit the market (the user's player has his own; his rival stays put). Best players first, his old team may bid with Bird rights over the cap (likelier for a good young player; rebuilding teams let veterans walk), and other teams bid with cap room or, once a summer, the mid-level exception, if they have a roster spot. He picks by money, then winning, then a role. Veterans nobody wants retire; the rest take the minimum where there's room. Moves make the news (big ones the Insider's feed too), and the Summer step lists the summer's signings.
- **Draft picks as trade assets**: every team owns its next four first-round picks unless it trades them, and a traded pick goes to its owner on draft night. A pick is worth more the worse the team that gave it up and less the further out it is. Rebuilding teams selling a veteran take back the buyer's furthest first; teams trading for the user's player add a first when their players fall short, and the offer shows it ("+ a 2029 first"), counted in the deal's verdict. The Team tab lists the team's picks.
- **Coaching and front-office changes**: each offseason losing teams fire coaches (most after a season under .350, some after missing the playoffs); the new coach brings his own system, an offense that fits his strengths and a defense, and resets the rotation. Bad teams also change general managers. The news, the Insider's feed and the Summer step report them; the user's team gets a pop-up, and the Team tab shows the coach's system.
- **Team salary caps**: every pro has a salary and years left (market value give or take, one to four years; draftees on the rookie scale, four years in the first round and two in the second; the user's player's comes from his contract). A team's payroll sits under the cap ($154.6M today, scaled to the era), over it, over the luxury tax line (121.5% of the cap) or over the first apron (126.8%); the Team tab shows the payroll, a bar to the tax line and the room left. Deals tick down each summer in free agency (see League-wide free agency). In free agency a team with cap room can offer up to it; a team over the cap only the mid-level exception (9.15% of the cap); the player's own team can go over the cap to keep him (Bird rights). Offers say which ("Cap space", "Mid-level exception", "Bird rights") when that's the limit. Teams over the cap have to match salaries in trades (taking back no more than 125% of what they send plus $100K), for the user's player and in the AI's deals. Player cards show a pro's contract.
- **Defense that shows**: the defender guarding the shooter (the one at his position, or the nearest) counts for 40% of the perimeter defense on his shot and 30% inside, with the team's average making up the rest, so a lockdown defender takes something away from his man. Steals go by steal and perimeter-defense ratings equally, and perimeter-defense styles get 60% more of them. The player card's defense category weighs what the position needs (perimeter defense and steals for guards and wings, interior defense and blocks for bigs), so a two-way wing reads like one.
- **Player card**: the Career tab splits NCAA Tournament games from pro playoff games, says the stat tables scroll sideways, and the Growth tab charts a pro's seasons (on the pro scale, by year) instead of stopping at his senior year. The header shows age and, for pros, the year in the league (rookie, 2nd year...); a strip under the ratings shows this season (points, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks, FG% and 3P%, with games and minutes); the play style comes with what it does on the floor ("Two-Way Guard: hunts threes, creates off the dribble, locks down the perimeter"). College players show their draft stock and mood; pros show their trade value (Franchise, All-Star, Starter, Rotation or Bench) instead. The Ratings tab opens with five categories (outside, inside, playmaking, defense, rebounding), and every rating shows on the same scale as the overall.
- **Me tab**: the Me tab, college and pro, splits into four pages instead of one long scroll: You (situation, award races, rival), Team (leading your team, your role), Growth (practice, staff, development) and Media (media, social, NIL). The NIL deal choice and the press card stay on top of every page. A happy player gets a quiet "Request a trade anyway" link instead of a big button.
- **Ratings**: pro ratings show on the 2K scale: about 20 players are 90 or better, a typical starter is about 77, a rookie about 70 (the screens convert from the game's internal pro scale, where the numbers in this README live: internal 83 shows as 75, 90 as 85, 95 as 92, 99 as 98). College ratings show as they are. Today's 522 real players carry their **NBA 2K27** cards from the user's full ratings file (`resources/eras/today2k.tsv`, made by `tools/rate_today_2k.py`): the overall, the potential (from the letter grade: A+ 97, A 93, A- 90, B+ 87, B 84, B- 81) and the shape of their 36 attributes on the game's ten ratings (Jokic passes like a point guard, Daniels steals everything). Pros grow slower than college players and mostly while young (a fifth to a third of the gap to their ceiling a summer at 22 and under, less after), so the league keeps about 2K's number of 90s: in a test, 18 at the start, no team with more than two, and 16 two seasons later. A player who grows past his ceiling raises it.
- **Play styles on the floor**: a player's style steers what he does in games, through the same three ratings it boosts. 3-point styles take about 60% more threes, mid-range styles more pull-ups, inside styles attack the rim; passing styles get about 80% more of the assists (handling adds a little); rebounding styles about 70% more of the boards (interior defense adds some); steal styles about 80% more of the steals, and perimeter-defense styles 45% more on top (so a Two-Way Guard, a 3-and-D Wing or a Ball Hawk gets his steals: steals go by steal and perimeter-defense ratings); block styles about 90% more of the blocks. Scoring styles look for their shot more and each defensive rating in a style a little less (a Lockdown Defender shoots about a fifth less than a Shot Creator at the same ratings). Once your player is clearly his team's best, the offense runs through him (up to 40% more touches) and he does more of everything his style does (up to about a quarter more of his rebounds, steals, blocks and assists), not just score. In the pros no one takes much more than a third of his team's shots, so the scoring leader lands around 31 to 35 a game.
- **Leading your team** (college and pros): a star changes games beyond his own shots. With your player on the floor, his teammates' shots get easier (defenses load up on him), by how far he's above the team's best seven (up to 12 points) and by his **leadership**. In a test, the same Mavericks team won 44 games without him, 50 with him at 88, 53 at 92 and 69 at 98. Leadership (0 to 100: New in the room, Finding his voice, Respected voice, Captain at 60, Franchise leader at 80) grows each season he starts, more when the team beats its preseason projection, and with humble answers to the press; brash answers cost some, and a trade or new team takes a third of it. The Me tab's **Leading your team** panel shows the team's projected wins against its pace ("+11 over the projection. You're carrying them."), his plus-minus a game (now tracked for every player: the team's points minus the other team's while he's on the floor, five players sharing every point), his leadership and the lift his teammates get. A season that beats the projection by six or more makes the news ("led the Hawks to 49 wins, 12 more than projected").
- **Earned growth**: your player's summer growth comes from his season, not the calendar. How much of the gap to his ceiling he closes depends on minutes (45%), production against what his rating predicts (35%), and practice (20%: extra shooting, film or weights count fully, team practice half, rest nothing, worn-out work half): about half the gap for a starter who plays above his rating and puts in extra work, about a tenth for a benchwarmer who rests. The summer screen and the Me tab say why ("+7 this summer: a starter's minutes (34 a game), played well above his rating, extra work most days"), and the Me tab's **How you're growing** shows the outlook mid-season. Practice still adds rating points during the season as before; after 30 he ages like everyone. Other players grow as before.
- **Rotations**: coaches play their best players. A backup who is clearly better than the starter at his own or the next position starts there, and a good player left out takes a weaker backup's minutes at a nearby position, so nobody good sits all season for being third at his spot. Teams make room for the young player they drafted to build around (you). Every season on a roster counts as a year in the league, played or not.
- **Two positions**: about a third of players also play a position next to their own (a PG/SG combo guard, an SF/PF forward, a PF/C big). Only next-door spots count (a guard can't be a second center), and a player gets one when his ratings at that spot are about as good as at his own. The pairs that suit the real game come up most: about 45% of power forwards also play forward or center, and about a quarter of centers also play the four. Rosters and the player page show both ("SG/PG"), and the depth chart picker lists players who can play the spot first. Coaches start or bring a player off the bench at either spot, so a team short on centers plays its PF/C there. You pick your player's second position when you create him (or None), and can change it in Edit player; he can then train either position's play styles. In a test league, 159 of 450 pros and 1,556 of 4,732 college players play two positions, and 45 pro rotation spots go to a player at his second position. `PositionTest` checks the shares, the pairs, the rotations and the save.
- **Box scores**: a pro season averages about 113 points, 89 shots at .47, 13 threes, 27 assists, 8 steals, 5 blocks, 45 rebounds (11 offensive) and 15 turnovers a team. Stats split across positions like the real game: starting point guards about 19 points, 3.5 rebounds and 7 assists; centers about 14 points, 9 rebounds, 2 assists, 1.5 blocks and a three every other game; power forwards about 7.5 rebounds. About one rebound in nine goes to the team (out of bounds, tie-ups) rather than a player, as in real box scores. Leaders land around 31 points, 12 rebounds, 9 to 10 assists, 2 to 3 blocks and 5 threes a game. College stays near real college numbers: about 74 points, 14 assists, 33 rebounds and 3 blocks a team, with point guards around 3 rebounds and 4 assists and centers around 7.5 rebounds. `StatsTest` plays a full season of each and checks team averages, leaders and every position.
- **Trades**: the Me tab shows your mood (winning, your minutes for your place on the team, and from 30 a shot at a ring) and why. Ask for a trade once a season before the deadline (day 40) or once in the offseason: your team's fans sour, your agent brings back up to three teams with the players they'd send, and you pick one or withdraw. A request left open is settled at the deadline (or when camp opens) with the first team that called. At the deadline your team may also move you without asking (rarely as a rookie or a young star; more often on a losing team, past 27, or behind better players). AI teams make 4 to 8 deals at the deadline and 6 to 10 each summer: sellers move a veteran 28 or older to a contender for younger pieces of similar value. Trade value climbs steeply with overall and favors youth and upside. Every trade is listed on the League tab's History page and in the offseason.
- **All-time list**: every career gets legacy points: MVP 8, Finals MVP 6, ring 5, All-League 3, 1.5 or 1, All-Star 1, Defensive Player of the Year 2, Rookie of the Year 1, Olympic gold 2, and 0.6 per 1,000 career points (seasons count as 83 games; 58-game seasons from older saves are scaled up). The list starts with about 50 retired greats scored from their real careers (estimates), plus today's stars carrying their real résumés, which keep growing as they play. Anyone who builds a career in this league joins it, and players who retire keep their spot. You join at 20 points. The Me tab shows your rank, the next name and how far away; the Career tab shows the top 15 and the names around you; Today and the news announce each legend you pass ("passes Kobe Bryant for No. 6 on the all-time list"), each name once. The Spotlight and MVP stories add a projected stat line.

NBA: the hottest and coldest teams over their last 10, who's ahead of and behind our preseason projection (on pace for how many), the scoring race, a rotating stat leader (assists, rebounds, blocks, three-point shooting) and the rookie to watch. College: the unbeatens, the hottest team, the poll's biggest crasher and our biggest preseason miss, the Player of the Year race, the freshman to watch and a stat leader.

  - *Big nights*: the story adds context, either how far above his normal the night was or the strength of the opponent he did it against.
- **Social feed in two tabs** (5.5): the Social screen (Today → Social → See all) has **You** (what people say about your career, as in 5.3) and **Around the league**. The league tab is built from the day's stories: the outlet posts the headline and its first lines, then the most-liked reactions follow (players, coaches, analysts, insiders, fans; three for the big ones like trades, series and upsets), each marked with the story it's about. Insider scoops on other teams' trades, signings, hires and deadline rumors land there too. **Trending** shows the three stories people engaged with most over the last few days. On Today, under your two latest posts, the newest league story links to the league tab. Older saves start filling the league tab as you play.

While he coaches in college, his NBA league is kept in the save. When he takes the job, it moves forward to the year he arrives: everyone ages and develops, the old guard retires, and a draft class arrives each year. - **Your feed and your press, about your career** (5.3):
  - *Only about you*: the social feed ("what people are saying about you") no longer fills up with the rest of the league. Other teams' trades, signings, coaching hires and rumors only show up when they involve your team. Real accounts are matched to the level: NBA networks never post about a college game, college networks never post about an NBA one, and first-person takes come from analysts who are people (not "ESPN" saying "I watched the tape").
  - *After a game*: the team's final names its top scorer and your line ("off the bench" when you are). Fans judge the night against your own normal: 12 points is a big night for a bench player and a bad one for a star. What they say depends on who you are: a rookie or freshman, a late pick ("No. 49 pick, by the way"), a sixth man, a career high, a game against the team that traded you or the school you left. Ordinary nights only get a post now and then. Doubters only bother with players worth doubting (a big contract, big NIL money, a star), and not on back-to-back nights. Analysts post the numbers behind a trend ("13.3 over his last five; before that, 9.9"), a few days apart. Your rival posts his side, with the real head-to-head. Nobody posts the same line twice in a row.
  - *Career moments*: the draft (the team's pick announcement, an analyst on the pick, your own post), trades, signings, extensions and transfers (welcome posts, your old fans saying goodbye, the insider with the terms), every honor (All-Star with the count, MVP, titles, All-NBA, statistical titles, All-Conference, All-American, medals, records), and career-point milestones (1,000, 5,000, 10,000...). Each gets posts from the team, the league, analysts, fans, your rival and you.
  - *Postgame press*: questions come from what's going on in your career, with your numbers in them. Examples: the first game against your old team, the head-to-head with your rival since high school, a career high against your old best, the series score, the season ending (or your college career, and whether you're coming back with the draft board where it is), the fit since a trade, the MVP or Rookie of the Year race and who's ahead, the All-Star vote, a hot week or a slump, the teams that passed on you in the draft, a contract year at your salary, being the highest-paid player, your draft-board rank and NIL deal in college, senior night, freshman or rookie adjustments, the playoff race, your minutes and role. The biggest story of the night wins; season storylines (contract year, draft stock, contender talk) come up once in a while, not every week. Your team's beat writer asks most questions and stays the same for years; national writers show up for the big ones.
- **Polish pass** (5.2):
  - *Charlotte*: today's league had the Charlotte Bobcats (a data error for 2025-26 and 2026-27); they're the Hornets again, with their logo, and 2013-14 is correctly the Bobcats' last season. Saves that already have the Bobcats rename at the next new season.
  - *News*: the line under each headline stays on one line (the byline shortens if it must), with the reaction count and "Read". Each writer works for one outlet. Datelines name the city ("BROOKLYN —", not "BROOKLYN NETS —"), college stories drop the stray "the" ("Kentucky beat Missouri", not "The Kentucky beat the Missouri"), and a scoring line leaves out zero rebounds or assists.
  - *NBA team pages*: the record line shows the conference record and place ("7-6 East · 7th in the East") instead of college poll numbers and a NET of 0; the subtitle says "Big market" or "Small market" instead of "Power program"; the fourth tab is Franchise. Pro rosters show each player's age instead of "PRO".
  - *Small fixes*: "1 Team", "1 Season" and "1 Title" on the NBA coach's Career tab; the trade finder says "Starter value" (a bench player can have one) instead of "Starter"; the college NET list shows a team's AP rank instead of repeating its own rank; "about 4th in the East" (not "EAST") in the NBA preview and Cup notes; your rival's overall uses the same scale as your own; in the NBA the rival card says you may meet in the playoffs, not in March.
- **Hidden longevity** (5.1): every pro has a hidden longevity rating (1 to 99, 50 average) for how well his body holds up. It's never shown. It moves the age his body plays at by up to four years either way, which decides when he starts to decline and when he retires: at 90 he plays like a man three years younger, at 15 he starts slipping at 28. Real players get theirs from their real careers: how old they were in their last real season (35 is average, 40 near the top), or, for those still playing in the data's last season, at least a year older than they are now (LeBron James, Al Horford near the top; Stephen Curry and Kevin Durant well above average). Everyone else's is drawn once from who he is and stays the same. Your player has one too; from 29 on, the training staff hints at it every summer ("holding up like a 25-year-old's", "the miles are starting to show").
- **NBA contract rules** (2025-26 numbers): a $154.6M salary cap. The max is 25% of the cap through six years in the league, 30% for seven to nine and 35% from ten, with the 35% supermax for a seven-to-nine-year player re-signing after an MVP, Defensive Player of the Year or All-League season in the last three. The minimum climbs from $1.27M for a rookie to $3.63M at ten years. Rookie scale: No. 1 about $13.8M a season down to about $2.4M at No. 30, four years; second-rounders two years near the minimum; undrafted players a two-way at half the minimum. Only your own team can offer five years; a new team stops at four. Coming off a first-round rookie deal you're a restricted free agent: sign an offer sheet elsewhere and your team may match it and keep you (likelier the better you are).
- **The 65-game rule**: MVP, Defensive Player, Sixth Man, Most Improved and the All-League and All-Defensive teams need 65 games. Rookie of the Year and All-Rookie don't. A test career ran 18 seasons, still a 94 overall at 36 and an All-Star at 38. League ratings stay steady from year to year.
- **Contract negotiation**: tap a free-agent offer to negotiate. Ask for 10% or 20% more once per offer: each team has a limit it won't show you, so it may agree, come up to its limit as a final offer, or walk away (more likely on a 20% ask). Pick the length (up to 5 years when you're young, fewer as you age). Add a player option on the last year for 5% less a season: the summer before that year you opt in, or opt out and test free agency again. With a contender (55% wins or a conference finals run), take a ring discount: sign for 15% less and the team spends the savings on a good veteran.
- **Big playoff nights**: Game 7s, elimination games and close-outs ("Win or go home", "Win and you're champions") get up to five big moments, starting at three minutes left and within seven points, plus a tag on Today and in the live game, more followers and their own headlines. A playoff highlight reel keeps every big night, 30-point game and game-winner.
- **Records book**: 18 real records (points, rebounds, assists, threes, steals and blocks in a game, per game in a season, and for a career) with the real holders, from Wilt's 100 to LeBron's points and Curry's threes. Anyone in the league can break them, and holders still playing keep adding. Career totals count 58-game seasons as 83. Breaking one makes the news and adds 3 legacy points; the Career tab shows each record with your progress.
- **Recruit a star**: title odds come from each team's best eight. Each summer four stars will hear a pitch (one per summer; the chance grows with your team's odds, your star power, the market, and older stars chasing rings). Before the deadline you can push the front office once a season to trade for a star on a losing team. Every target shows your title odds with him.
- **Jersey night and the Hall of Fame**: when you retire, every team you starred for (three seasons with an All-Star or a ring there, or six seasons) retires your number, and a Hall of Fame career gets an induction night four years on, listing your highlights, presented by your old rival.
- **Life after playing**: retired, take a college coaching job (your alma mater first, others by your legacy). It starts a new save in the coach game with your name, your age, and coaching ratings drawn from your career (recruiting from your legacy, offense and defense from your peak).
- **One career: player → college coach → NBA coach**: your playing career travels with you. Retire from the NBA and take a college job; win there and NBA teams call; take one and you're an NBA head coach. Or skip college and take an NBA bench the day you retire. A player who goes undrafted can coach too: after years overseas (until about 31) he comes home to a smaller program (his old school if he was a star there).
  - *Your résumé goes with you*: the college and NBA Career tabs show **Your path** (each stop with its years and record: player in college, NBA player or overseas, college coach, NBA coach) and **As a player** (schools, college line and honors, draft slot, NBA teams, pro line, rings, MVPs, All-Star and All-NBA selections, Hall of Fame verdict and all-time rank).
  - *Your name opens NBA doors*: a former NBA star (an MVP, a Hall of Famer, five All-Star games or an all-time great) gets NBA calls after a Sweet 16 run about a third of the time, and after smaller tournament runs now and then; a solid pro (an All-Star, a ring or eight seasons) a little more often than a coach who never played. The last NBA team you played for that's in the league that year often calls first ("Your old team wants you back on its bench").
  - *Hall of Fame night*: if your playing career earned a Hall of Fame class, you're inducted that year wherever you're coaching (news and a pop-up).
  - *Straight to an NBA bench*: retire from the NBA and teams call about their head-coaching job the same day, listed on the ending screen above the college jobs. Losing teams looking for a new voice: one offer for a role player, two for a solid pro (an All-Star, a ring or eight seasons), three for a legend (who can land a good team too). The team you finished with often wants you (25%, 50% or 80% by your name). Take one and the league you played in becomes your coaching career, picking up the offseason where you retired: same players, rivals and history, then the draft, free agency and your first season. Your ratings come from your playing career (offense and defense from your peak, player relations from your legacy), and your playing career goes on your Career tab.
- **The end**: retire when you choose from 30 on, or when nobody offers a contract (at 43 at the latest). The ending shows your pro and college totals, rings, MVPs, All-Star selections, earnings, a Hall of Fame verdict and how your rival's career went.

## Road to the League (experiment)

The `road-to-the-league` branch adds a second way to play: instead of coaching a program, you play one player's college career. It builds as a separate app, **Hoops Dynasty Road** (`com.nh.hoopscoach.road`), so it installs next to the main game and never touches its save. The main game keeps going on its own branch.

- **Create your player**: name, position, height and weight, play style, hometown and state, jersey number, and prospect level (3-star sleeper, 4-star prospect or 5-star blue-chipper). Every level has a real ceiling: 96 to 99 potential.
- **Scholarship offers**: eight schools offer, matched to your level, with a lean toward home. Each offer shows your projected role, how many players at your position are already as good as you, distance from home and the NIL money the school pays.
- **Your team is coached by the AI**: the coach sets the rotation and tactics, recruits and pays the roster. You see the roster and the program; your row is marked YOU. Minutes come from being the best at your position.
- **The Me tab**: draft stock and big-board rank, your role and planned minutes, the depth chart at your position, training focus and XP, your NIL deal and career earnings, and your story season by season. Today shows a "Your player" card with your last game.
- **NIL deals**: each season pick one. A hometown dealership pays full value. A performance deal pays 60% plus 4% per point you average and a bonus for All-America honors. A national brand pays 150% but costs 15% of your development that season. Richer programs pay more.
- **The offseason** in four steps:
  1. **Decision**: return to school, declare for the draft (undrafted underclassmen go back to school), or enter the transfer portal.
  2. **Draft Night**. If you're in the draft (you declared, or you're a senior), you wait in the green room while the picks come in one at a time: slow through the lottery, quicker through the first round, fast in the second. The room talks about each pick (a teammate going, another player at your position off the board), and your phone rings right before your name is called. Skip ahead any time. Then you see your pick against your mock-draft spot (your big-board rank among the players actually in the draft), or that the night ended without your name. The other tabs stay spoiler-free until the night is over.
  3. **Summer**: workouts, and transfer offers if you entered the portal.
  4. **Next season**: everything else (coaching changes, recruiting, tryouts) runs on its own.
- **Media and followers**: you start with a following that matches your ranking (about 150K for a top five-star, 50K to 70K for a four-star, 10K for a three-star). Big games grow it, more on big stages (NCAA Tournament x3, conference tournaments and rivalries x1.5, ranked opponents x1.3) and at big-brand schools. Thirty-point nights and game-winners go viral. Every follower adds $1 a year to your NIL value, and sponsors pay one-time bonuses at 100K, 250K, 500K, 1M, 2.5M and 5M followers.
- **Postgame press**: after notable games (a big night, a game-winner, a rivalry win, a tournament win, a tough loss, draft buzz) you get a question on Today and the Me tab with three answers. Humble answers please fans and teammates. Brash ones grow your following faster but cost locker-room goodwill. Your answers shape a persona: Fan favorite (wins lift the fan base more), Quiet pro, or Lightning rod (big games grow your following 20% faster).
- **Big moments**: in live games, when your team has the ball in the last two minutes and it's within five, the game pauses (up to three times a game). Pick the play: a pull-up jumper or three, a drive to the rim, or a kick-out to your best shooter. Each shows its chance to score from your ratings against that defense. Then the engine plays it out, with fouls, turnovers and rebounds. Simmed games play on their own. The Me tab counts how often you came through.
- **Weekly practice**: choose a plan, applied after every game.
  - Team practice: works your training focus.
  - Shooting reps: Inside, Mid-Range and 3-Point.
  - Film study: Handling, Passing and Perimeter D. Easy on the legs.
  - Weight room: Inside, Rebound and Interior D. The most tiring.
  - Rest: recovers fatigue.
  Practice work banks toward each rating (+1 per 300). Fatigue comes from practice and minutes. Past 40 it costs you up to 9% shooting in games, and past 70 practice gains are halved.
- **Spend your NIL**: the guaranteed part of your NIL deal lands in your bank when you sign. Performance bonuses arrive at season end, and sponsor payouts as you hit milestones. Spend the bank on your own team for the season (contracts end with it):
  - Personal trainer ($150K): develop 25% faster from games and practice.
  - Nutritionist ($90K): fatigue builds a quarter slower, and you recover faster.
  - Shooting coach ($120K): +3% on jump shots, +5% when you take the big shot late.
  - Agent ($80K): 15% more on NIL deals, bonuses and sponsor payouts.
  The Me tab shows your bank and staff; the career summary shows what you invested.
- **Spotlight**: live games jump from one of your plays to the next (your shots, assists, steals, fouls and turnovers, highlighted in the feed), so a game takes about a minute at 2x. Big moments still stop the game. Switch to "Every play" any time; the choice is saved.
- **Your class rival**: when your career starts (or, for a career saved before this update, when your next season starts), a five-star at your position (a top-five recruit in your class) signs with one of the best programs in your conference, so you meet at least twice a season. The Me tab puts him side by side with you: overall, scoring, the Player of the Year race and the big board, plus your head-to-head record and the next meeting. Today flags rival games, live games show both of your point totals, winning the duel grows your following faster, and the press asks about him. On Draft Night the room calls out his pick, and your ending says how you came out against him.
- **Summer events**: each summer after the draft, pick one invitation. Elite camps (a guard, wing or bigs camp for your position, a shooting camp and a lockdown defense camp) add +2 to three ratings and some followers, with a chance at camp MVP. After your first two seasons, a top-40 big-board rank or an 84 overall gets you a U19 National Team invite: every rating +1, a medal and a big jump in followers. Your summers show up in your story.
- **Award races**: a Player of the Year ladder on the Me tab, scored the same way as the end-of-season awards. It shows your rank, your All-America pace, a weekly up or down arrow, your conference race and the freshman race.
- **The ending** (on the Road branch): drafted (with your pick) or, after four seasons, undrafted and overseas. On this branch the draft leads to the pros instead. You get a career summary with totals, your story, NIL earned and every school you played for.

## Install on your phone

1. Open the repository's **Releases** page on your Android phone and download `HoopsDynasty.apk` from the newest build. Every push builds a fresh one.
2. Open the file. If Android asks, allow your browser or file manager to install unknown apps.
3. Your dynasty saves automatically on the device.

**Keep your save safe:** In **Settings → Back Up Save**, write your dynasty to a file. If Android ever refuses to install a new build over the old one ("app not installed" or a package conflict), it means the builds were signed with different keys. Back up first, uninstall, install the new build, then use **Restore Save**.

## What's in the game

- **Continue bar**: one bar above the tabs always shows what's next. Play the game live, sim it, or use the ⋮ menu to jump a week, to the conference tournament, to Selection Sunday, or to the end of the season.
- **Today**: next opponent with win odds and a head-to-head comparison, recent form, conference standings, top performers and headlines. During tournaments it shows your road to the title and the current round.
- **Team**
  - *Roster*: starting five, rotation and bench with jersey numbers, rating rings and personality traits.
  - *Lineup*: offense and defense styles, starter and backup at each position, a minutes slider, and optional usage tuning.
  - *Develop*: XP progress and a training focus (archetype) for each player.
  - *Program*: Prestige, Legacy, Buzz, Program Pull, Conference Exposure, Expected Wins and the resume ceiling, plus NIL budget, locker-room mood and coaching staff.
  - The **i** button opens the **Program Guide**, with tabs for Players, NIL, Gameplan, Training and Program.
- **Recruit**: a class of 1,600 high-school prospects plus the transfer portal. In season you scout and build your targets; after the season, recruiting runs in five rounds (details below). The **i** button opens the **Recruiting Guide**.
- **Program numbers**: Prestige (0-100) is the durable brand and moves once a season.
  - **How it moves**: about 22% of the way toward what the season earned, plus 4 more for each season in a row it went up (up to three), so a program on a run climbs faster; a season well above your standing is always worth at least +2 (+1 for a smaller gap). On the way down it slides 14%. A season earns credit for record, conference finish and tournament title, NCAA run, Quadrant 1 wins, last summer's draft picks and national awards. Rivalry games and an NIT title add on top. Legacy follows a rising program faster, so climbs stick.
  - **Seeing it move**: during the season the Program panel shows what the season is on pace to earn, the change that would bring, and what a Sweet 16 run would add. The season review breaks down what the season earned piece by piece (record, conference, national stage, March, quality wins, draft picks, awards) and how far Prestige moved toward it.
  - **Conference resistance**: records in a low-exposure league count for less (78% at the lowest), and a high-exposure league adds a little; a low-exposure league takes away less than before. Below 70 records and titles drive the climb; above 70 it takes March.
  - **Resume ceiling**: above 70, the last 10 NCAA Tournaments set a soft ceiling (Round of 32 most years supports the low 80s, Sweet 16 most years the high 80s, one Elite Eight the high 80s on its own, a Final Four or title 90 and up). Past it, climbing slows to 40%, and above 80 Prestige drifts back toward it. Years before the save began count at the level Legacy implies.
  - **Legacy**: the long-run baseline. It moves a few points a decade and starts higher for programs with big pasts (UCLA, Indiana, Georgetown and others). It cushions a fallen power, lets it recover faster, and slows a one-year spike.
  - **Buzz**: short-term heat from ranked wins, upsets, rivalry wins, conference tournament titles and March wins (Cinderella runs count 50% more). It cools every game and halves over the summer.
  - **Conference Exposure**: a league's national visibility, from its level and its members' prestige.
  - **Program Pull**: 45% Prestige, 15% Legacy and 10% each of Conference Exposure, coach recruiting, recent NCAA results and Buzz. Recruits see it as Recruiting Pull.
  - **Expected Wins**: the preseason target from your schedule and team strength.
  - The season review lists everything that moved your Prestige, including draft picks, Legacy and the resume ceiling.
- **Recruiting cycle**: five rounds in the offseason, after the NIL budget step.
  - **Budget**: one point budget for the whole cycle, from your Recruiting Pull and your coach's recruiting rating (plus 10% for an Ace Recruiter).
  - **Rounds**: before each round, rival schools set their points. You put points on prospects (they stay there until you move them), preview where you'd stand, and lock in. Points have diminishing returns, and gains taper as interest nears 100.
  - **Interest**: how much your points count depends on program strength (your Pull against what he's after), coach recruiting, location (in-state and same region), pro pipeline, playing time, roster crowding at his position, and his own preference: close to home, early playing time, winning now, a big-name program, NIL money or a path to the pros. Each prospect page rates every factor for your school.
  - **NIL offers**: an offer adds interest every round on its own, even with no points on him (about +8 a round at his ask, +13 at double, 60% more for NIL-minded prospects and Bag Chasers). Rival programs offer 60% to 120% of the ask depending on their NIL budget. All your open offers must fit next year's NIL room, and the prospect page shows the room left and exactly what your offer adds.
  - **Locking in**: interest moves exactly as the preview showed. Then some prospects commit (leaders with high interest and a clear margin, more often in later rounds), points on them come back, schools that fall far behind or run out of scholarships drop out, and new schools enter. A round report shows what happened on your board.
  - **End of cycle**: prospects with a clear favorite sign late, programs with room pick up some of the rest, and the others go unsigned. Your open spots go to walk-on tryouts.
  - **Auto-assign**: one tap plans the round for you: it steps out of races that are lost, keeps about two prospects per open scholarship, matches NIL asks while the room lasts, and puts most of your free points where the chances are best (never more than 30% of the budget on one prospect). Simming the offseason does this every round.
  - **Commitments**: once a prospect's interest is high he may decide. He picks among the schools near the top of his race: a tie is a coin flip, and a school 5 points back is about a third as likely as the leader.
- **League**: AP Top 25 and NET, standings for all 32 conferences, stat leaders, Bubble Watch, bracketology and brackets, and history.

  - **Stats**: a season-by-season table (minutes, points, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks, turnovers and shooting) that switches between per-game and totals, with the season in progress highlighted and a career line. Plus his NCAA Tournament stats, and career highs with the game each one came in.
  - **Honors by season**: each season shows his team, its record and result (Sweet 16, NIT Champion and so on), and every honor: All-American teams, national and conference awards, All-Tournament, draft position, walk-on tryouts and program Hall of Fame.
  - **Former players**: when players leave, their career pages stay on record. That covers everyone who played for you, every draft pick and every All-American. Open them from the Former players list on team pages, the draft history, award winners and the Hall of Fame.
- **Live games**: possession-by-possession play-by-play, a live win-probability chart, speed controls and in-game tactics.
- **Season structure**: 31-game regular season with in-season tournaments, conference tournaments, a 68-team NCAA Tournament and a 32-team NIT.
  - **Selection**: 32 automatic bids and 36 at-large. The committee ranks resumes by NET and Wins Above Bubble equally, with credit for Quad 1 wins and a penalty for Quad 4 losses. Bubble Watch uses the same formula, so its projection matches the real selection.
  - **First Four**: the last four at-large teams play for two 11 seeds, and the four lowest automatic qualifiers play for two 16 seeds, before the Round of 64.
  - **NIT**: the 32 best teams that missed the field, in four brackets of eight. Higher seeds host until the semifinals. The NIT champion gets +2 to +3 prestige, 3 extra coaching points and 350 XP for every returning player.
- **Season wrap-up**: results, awards (Player of the Year, All-Americans, All-Tournament), graduations, program prestige changes, and coach development points.
- **Offseason**, step by step:
  1. **Roster & NIL**: pitch draft entrants to return, raise below-market deals, release players.
  2. **Draft Night**: 60 picks.
  3. **Career Decision**: job offers from other programs. Win more and bigger schools call. Lose too much and you can get fired.
  4. **Player Development**: summer growth for every returning player.
  5. **NIL Budget**: donor pool, conference revenue share and performance bonuses.
  6. **Recruiting**: five rounds for the recruiting class and the transfer portal, then signing day.
  7. **Walk-on tryouts**: five unrated students try out. Instead of ratings you see drill grades for shooting, athleticism and the scrimmage, plus your coach's gut feeling. You can take a closer look at two of them to see their overall and get sharper grades. Keep up to two walk-ons on top of your 13 scholarships, and any scholarship you leave open can go to a walk-on too. Walk-ons cost no NIL money.
     - Once in a while a walk-on is a hidden gem. He breaks out over a summer, earns the Late Bloomer trait, develops faster and can become a star. The coach's gut is your best clue.
     - Other programs find gems too, so watch the headlines for "from walk-on to star" stories.
     - When a scholarship opens, award one to a walk-on from his player card.

## Signing key

The signing key is never committed. Local builds read it from `keystore.properties` in the project root (git-ignored):

```properties
storeFile=/path/to/your.keystore
storePassword=...
keyAlias=...
keyPassword=...
```

GitHub Actions uses repository secrets instead: `KEYSTORE_BASE64` (the keystore file, base64-encoded), `KEYSTORE_PASSWORD`, `KEY_ALIAS` and `KEY_PASSWORD`. With them set, every published APK carries the same signature and installs over the last one. Without them, builds are signed with a throwaway debug key, so you'd need to back up your save, uninstall, and reinstall when switching builds.

Make a key with:

```sh
keytool -genkeypair -keystore hoops.keystore -alias hoops -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000
base64 -w0 hoops.keystore   # paste the output into the KEYSTORE_BASE64 secret
```
